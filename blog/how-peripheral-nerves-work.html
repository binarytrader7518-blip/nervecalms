How Your Peripheral Nerves Work: A Plain-English Guide

A plain-English guide to your peripheral nerves: what they do, why they can become irritated, and when tingling or discomfort needs a doctor.

By the NerveCalm Editorial TeamUpdated November 23, 20266 min read
In this article

  2. Why nerves can become irritated
  3. Signs worth paying attention to
  4. Where supplements fit in

Your nervous system is often described as the body's wiring. The brain and spinal cord form the central system. Everything that branches out from them, to your hands, feet, skin, muscles and organs, makes up the peripheral nervous system. Understanding the basics makes it much easier to judge any nerve-support product, NerveCalm included, with clear eyes.

The three jobs of peripheral nerves

  • Sensory nerves carry information about touch, temperature, vibration and discomfort back to the brain.
  • Motor nerves send signals from the brain to your muscles so you can walk, grip and move.
  • Autonomic nerves quietly manage functions such as heart rate, digestion and sweating.

The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke explains that peripheral neuropathy is an umbrella term covering more than 100 conditions in which these nerves are damaged. Symptoms depend on which type of nerve is affected.

Why nerves can become irritated

Nerves need a steady supply of blood, oxygen and nutrients. Federal health resources point to several common contributors to nerve problems: long-term high blood glucose, certain vitamin deficiencies (especially B12), heavy alcohol use, physical compression, injuries, infections and some medications. The NIDDK notes that diabetes is among the most frequent causes, because high blood glucose can harm nerves and the small blood vessels that feed them over time.

Signs worth paying attention to

  • Tingling, "pins and needles," or numbness that keeps coming back
  • Burning or sharp sensations, often in the feet or hands
  • Weakness, clumsiness or balance problems
  • Sensitivity to light touch

These symptoms can have many causes, and some are treatable when caught early. That is why ongoing symptoms should always be checked by a doctor rather than self-managed with supplements alone.

Where supplements fit in

Dietary supplements are regulated differently from medicines. The FDA does not approve them for safety or effectiveness before they are sold. Some people still choose botanical formulas, such as NerveCalm, as one part of a broader routine. A sensible approach looks like this:

  1. Get a diagnosis for persistent symptoms.
  2. Address the basics: blood sugar, nutrition, movement and sleep.
  3. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before adding any supplement, especially if you take medication.
  4. Give any product a fair, consistent trial and track how you feel.
